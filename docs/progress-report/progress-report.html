The Original Question

Progression in a massively multiplayer online role-playing game such as RuneScape is a long-horizon, multi-objective planning problem. Skills unlock income, income buys equipment that speeds up training, and an early investment may pay off hours of game time later. The proposal (Griffin, 2026a) set out to train agents that choose whole activities in 2009Scape (2009Scape Project, n.d.), a free, open-source recreation of RuneScape as it was in 2009, and to study how their strategies emerge, shift and differ across reward definitions and algorithms. Its main question was “Given a fixed game-time budget, how do learned high-level policies compare with scripted and greedy strategies at accumulating skills and wealth, and how do their strategies emerge during training?” (Griffin, 2026a, p. 1). It divided the question into five research questions (RQs):

  • RQ1 (performance). Can a learned policy exceed greedy XP-per-hour, greedy GP-per-hour, and a scripted guide-style routine on total level and bank value at fixed game-time checkpoints?
  • RQ2 (strategy emergence). In what order does the agent discover activities, and does it learn to invest early (for example, training a skill that unlocks better income later)? Does this appear gradually or as a sudden change in the learning curve?
  • RQ3 (reward design). How does the weighting between skill and wealth rewards shape behavior and the achievable trade-off frontier? Can a single preference-conditioned agent match a set of separately trained agents?
  • RQ4 (exploration and credit assignment). Do an exploration bonus, the discount factor, and potential-based shaping change which strategies are found and how quickly?
  • RQ5 (exploratory, time permitting). Do learned low-level options (click-ahead pathing, prayer flicking) improve high-level results over scripted versions? (Griffin, 2026a, pp. 1–2)

Here XP is experience points and GP is gold pieces, the game's currency. The proposal expected three outcomes:

(H1) Learned policies will beat greedy baselines mainly by front-loading investments whose payoff is delayed. (H2) Different reward weightings will produce visibly different activity mixes rather than the same strategy at different speeds. (H3) An exploration bonus will change the order in which activities are discovered. (Griffin, 2026a, p. 2)

It set its own test of success: the project succeeds “if at least one learned configuration outperforms the strongest greedy baseline on a target metric with non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals across seeds, or if I can document and analyze why it does not” (Griffin, 2026a, p. 5).

What Was Built

The Game Server and the Decision Gate

I forked the 2009Scape server (AGPLv3) and added a reinforcement learning (RL) bridge to it: a local socket interface that spawns a character, reads its full state and drives it through the game's own player-facing paths (the bank, shops and the Grand Exchange [GE], the game's player market). The game's rules treat the character as a human player.

The game runs on a clock of ticks, and every action, walking step and timer is counted in them. By default, as in the original game, a tick lasts 600 ms, so 100 ticks are one minute of game time and 6,000 ticks are one game-hour; at this default, one game-hour takes one hour on the clock. That default is the reference for every speed below: “real time” means one tick every 600 ms. The first week's decision gate had three checks:

  • Speed (passed). The tick length can be set while the server runs. With 100 ambient bots in the world, the server asked for 120 ms ticks ran at 123 ms a tick, 4.9 times real time, and asked for 15 ms ticks, it ran at 17.6 ms, 34 times real time.
  • State and actions (passed). The bridge reads the character's full state and drives the game's own interactions.
  • The Grand Exchange (answered: static). The market is stocked by bots and its prices never move: a 10-hour warm-up changed none of its 7,324 prices. So net worth is valued at a fixed price table: each item's January 2009 GE price (Weird Gloop, n.d.), or its high-alchemy value less a nature rune where that is higher.

A Calibrated Simulator

Training takes millions of decisions, far more than the server can play. So the agents train on an event-level simulator of the game. Its skilling, combat and drop formulas are ported from the server's source code, and the overheads (walking, banking, trading, clicking) are measured on the server. Every kind of option was then checked against the server in the calibration gate: 533 of 548 measured metrics are within ±20%, over 236 cases in 11 families, and each of the 15 outside is explained (Table S11 in the supporting results). The calibration ran the server at 50 ms ticks, 12 times real time. Because the simulator counts ticks, not seconds, the tick length should change only how long a run takes on the clock, not what happens in it. A speed check confirmed this by rerunning cases at the default 600 ms: for gathering, combat and walking, all 18 of its cases agreed (experience rates within 8%, and every walk the same to the tick but one), and later checks for Agility, Crafting, Herblore and Runecrafting passed as well. The simulator was then frozen for the experiments.

The Formulation as Built

Table 1 gives the RL formulation as it was built. The agent is a high-level policy over options, in the semi-Markov decision process (semi-MDP) sense of Sutton et al. (1999): each choice runs for a variable stretch of game time.

Table 1

The Reinforcement Learning Formulation as Built

ComponentAs built
AgentA high-level policy that chooses options; each option's low-level steps are scripted.
EpisodeA fresh level-3 character with the starter kit, for 20 game-hours (120,000 ticks), with checkpoints every 2 game-hours. Quests are out of scope: all 68 are complete from the start, as flags only.
Actions2,294 options: an activity at a site (“chop logs and fletch them,” “pickpocket Master Farmers,” “fight cows”), banking, buying in shops, and buying or selling on the GE. An action mask hides the options whose requirements are not met (Huang & Ontañón, 2022).
Observation3,488 features: levels and experience, inventory, bank and equipment, location, time left, and features of each option.
Rewardr = u · Δ(total level) / 323.87 + (1 − u) · Δ(net worth) / 1,763,863 gp. Each objective is in units of the best 20-hour gain any baseline made on it (the scripted guide's levels, greedy GP/hr's wealth), so (.5, .5) weights them equally. The weighting (u, 1 − u), written (skill, wealth), is the experimental variable.
DiscountSemi-MDP: a reward τ ticks after the decision is discounted by γhrτ / 6,000, a discount per game-hour, with each decision counted as at least 100 ticks. γhr = 0.99 unless an experiment varies it.
LearnerMasked PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) from sb3-contrib, the companion library of Stable-Baselines3 (Raffin et al., 2021), with a semi-MDP rollout buffer: generalized advantage estimation (GAE; Schulman et al., 2016) with γτ. GAE λ = 0.95, learning rate 3 × 10−4 and entropy coefficient 0.01, chosen by the sanity check (E2). 2 million decisions a run.
EvaluationEach trained policy (sampling its actions) plays the same 30 seeded episodes as the baselines. Each configuration has five training seeds; results are means over the seeds with bootstrap 95% CIs.

Note. The four baselines are random play, greedy XP per hour, greedy GP per hour and a scripted guide that follows the RuneScape Wiki's January 2009 training guides.

Findings So Far

Every number below is the mean over five training seeds, each evaluated on the same 30 seeded 20-hour episodes, with a bootstrap 95% CI in brackets. The supporting results have every table in full.

RQ1: Beating the Baselines at the Ends of the Weighting, Not Yet in the Middle

The all-skill agents reach a total level of 382, 95% CI [363, 393], above the scripted guide's 351 [344, 357], the best baseline on level (Table 4). The all-wealth agents reach 3.74M gp [3.16M, 4.06M], about twice greedy GP/hr's 1.76M [1.67M, 1.85M]. In both cases the 95% CIs do not overlap the baseline's, which is the proposal's test of success. Both lead from the first checkpoint (Figure 1): at hour 2, 121 levels against at most 106 for a baseline, and 247k gp against at most 108k.

On the reward each agent was trained for, its weighted value, the ends win as well: (1, 0) scores 1.09 against the guide's 1.00 (four of five seeds above it), and (0, 1) 2.12 against greedy GP/hr's 1.00 (five of five). At the three mixed weightings, though, the best baseline scores higher on average: (.75, .25) scores 0.75 against the guide's 0.77, (.5, .5) 0.53 against greedy GP/hr's 0.57, and (.25, .75) 0.45 against 0.79. At (.5, .5), a shorter discount horizon beats the baseline in every seed (RQ4).

RQ2: Discovery Order, and Gradual or Sudden Learning

An activity counts as discovered in a training run when it first takes at least 5% of the game time in a block of 100,000 decisions. At (.5, .5), woodcutting, agility and melee are discovered in the first block, fishing by 400,000–500,000 decisions and thieving by 500,000–600,000 in all five runs, and banking by 0.8M–1.4M in four (Figure 2, which also shows the exploration bonus's runs, for H3). Learning is gradual at the skill end (all five runs at (1, 0), four of five at (.75, .25)) and sudden at the wealth end (all five at (0, 1)); at (.5, .5), one run is gradual, two are sudden and two show no gain. Whether the agents invest early is H1, below.

RQ3: The Weighting, the Trade-Off Frontier, and One Agent Against Five

From all skill to all wealth, the mean level at hour 20 falls from 382 to 41, and net worth rises from 11k to 3.74M gp. The activity mix moves with the weighting (Figure 3). At (1, 0) it is spread over melee, woodcutting, fishing and crafting (15–22% of game time each). At (.25, .75) it is woodcutting (97%), and at (0, 1) it is buying uncut gems in a shop and selling them on the GE (93%). Woodcutting here counts every woodcutting option, and the agents do choose what happens to the logs: each option either keeps the logs, burns them where they are cut (Firemaking experience) or cuts them into arrow shafts or bows with a knife (Fletching experience). At (1, 0), woodcutting takes about a fifth of the game time: the agents burn about two thirds of what they chop and fletch the rest (14% and 8% of the game time), and almost never keep the logs. At (.25, .75), they mostly chop normal logs and cut them into arrow shafts (92% of the game time), which stack in one inventory slot and are valued at 5 gp each; for another 4% they chop yew logs and keep them. Arrow shafts are 94% of these agents' net worth at hour 20, and yew logs 5%. These shares come from replaying the evaluation episodes: all 150 at (.25, .75), and those of four of the five runs at (1, 0).

One weight-conditioned agent (E5) covers about the same front as five separately trained ones (Figure 4; Roijers et al., 2013): a hypervolume of 0.394 [0.360, 0.456] against 0.373 [0.278, 0.484], for a fifth of the training (2.0M decisions and 8.4 hours a front, against 10.0M and 38.1). It falls short at the ends: told “all skill,” it reaches level 359, against the specialists' 382, and told “all wealth,” 862k gp, against 3.74M.

RQ4: The Exploration Bonus, the Discount and Shaping at (.5, .5)

RQ4 compares three changes with plain PPO at (.5, .5): RND's exploration bonus (Burda et al., 2019; E4), shorter discount horizons (E6a) and PBRS (Ng et al., 1999; E6b). All three change what is found and how fast (Figure 5 and Table 5). The exploration bonus ends lower on both objectives, and none of its learning curves gains. A shorter horizon learns faster and moves toward wealth: every run at γhr = 0.9 and 0.5 reaches the best baseline's value in training, and both beat it in every seed in evaluation; two of the seeds at γhr = 0.5 find the gem trade (about 4M gp each). Potential-based shaping ends lower too, and none of its curves gains; its fifth seed's evaluation episodes stop at the evaluator's cap of 100,000 decisions before hour 20, so it has four seeds at hour 20.

RQ5: Learned Low-Level Options, Bounded

RQ5 was “time permitting” and is answered by a bound, not an experiment. A perfect learned click-ahead could save at most the scripted walker's overhead beyond the shortest walk: 2.6% [1.4%, 4.0%] of the game time at (.5, .5), and 10.5% at most, for (0, 1), whose agents spend 96% of their game time walking. The scripted prayer flick never drains prayer and gets the prayer's full effect, so a learned flick cannot beat it.

The Hypotheses

H1, Investing Early: Partly Supported. The demonstration's (.5, .5) run invests, in one episode: it pickpockets its Thieving up to 38, the level Master Farmers need, by hour 7.9 while worth 38k gp, then passes a million gp by hour 20. But that is not how the agents win where they win. At both ends they lead from the first checkpoint, and the lead grows; the all-wealth agents gain 6 levels in 20 hours, and their win is the gem trade from the start.

H2, Different Mixes Rather Than One Strategy at Different Speeds: Partly Supported. On the wealth side the weighting changes the strategy; on the skill side it is one strategy (Figure 3). (1, 0) and (.75, .25) have nearly the same mix, and the Jensen–Shannon divergence between their seeds' mixes (0.138 bits) is less than between (.75, .25)'s own seeds (0.194). (.25, .75) and (0, 1) each have their own mix, which their seeds agree on (0.020 and 0.015 bits), 0.51–0.95 bits from every other weighting.

H3, the Bonus Changes the Discovery Order: Supported. RND changes which activities are discovered, and when (Figure 2). “Discovered” has RQ2's meaning: an activity first takes at least 5% of the game time in a 100,000-decision block of training. So an activity a run does only rarely is never discovered, though it happens. All five RND runs discover buying and selling, by 100,000–500,000 decisions; plain PPO discovers buying in none of its five runs and selling in one. Plain PPO still banks, buys and sells, but too little to count: in evaluation it spends 21% of its game time banking (partly in the banking loops described under Problems and Limitations), 1.4% buying and 0.1% selling. It does not need to sell to grow its net worth, because net worth counts coins and every item held, in the inventory, the bank or worn, at its price. In one evaluation episode of each of its five runs, its wealth at hour 20 is mostly what it has banked: in two runs, seeds pickpocketed from Master Farmers (a snapdragon seed is valued at 37,500 gp and a ranarr seed at 22,700 gp), and in the other three, arrow shafts fletched from logs are the largest part. RND, by contrast, spends 18% of its game time buying and 18% selling, and it never discovers fishing or banking, which plain PPO discovers in five and four of its five runs. The change does not pay (RQ4).

Problems and Limitations

  • Some runs end training in a loop. At γhr = 0.99, 10 of 40 runs end training repeating near-instant options that earn nothing (changing which loot to pick up, or banking and withdrawing), thousands of times an episode: eight of E3's 25 (four of five at (.5, .5)) and two of E6b's five. None of the 10 runs at γhr = 0.5 or 0.9 loops. The cause is untested; the final report will discuss it as a limitation.
  • The mixed weightings are sensitive to the trainer. Before the November 15 fix, E3's first round found the gem trade in five of its 15 mixed-weight runs; the rerun found it in none. At (.25, .75), the first round ended at 3.36M gp and level 123, against the rerun's 0.82M and 168. Why is untested.
  • Everything is in the simulator. It is checked against the server option by option, not by replaying whole episodes there.
  • Five training seeds leave wide intervals where the seeds disagree, such as at (.5, .5).
  • Scope. Quests are out, and the low-level actions are scripted, not learned (RQ5 is a bound).
